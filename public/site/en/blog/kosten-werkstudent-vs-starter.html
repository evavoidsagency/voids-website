WHAT DOES A WORKING STUDENT COST COMPARED TO A FULL-TIME STARTER?

Lower salary isn't the whole story: employer costs and risk differ too. A fair breakdown, including what the wrong choice ends up costing you.

This article is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Rules change and your situation may differ: check the current rules with an official source or a qualified advisor before relying on it.

A working student costs a company less in total than a full-time starter, because you buy fewer hours. That isn't the whole story: employer costs, flexibility and risk differ too, and those decide whether a working student is cost-efficient for your situation. Always check amounts and percentages at the source.

What's the direct cost difference between a working student and a full-time starter?

The biggest difference is in hours. An illustration at the minimum wage of € 14.99 gross per hour (as of 1 July 2026, from age 21 [1]): 20 hours a week is € 299.80 gross per week, 40 hours is € 599.60, excluding holiday pay and employer costs. The hourly rate needn't be lower; you just buy less time.

Are employer costs also lower for a working student?

Largely proportionally, but not entirely. The government's business portal lists as mandatory costs, among others, the minimum wage, at least 8% holiday pay and the payroll taxes: contributions for national insurance and employee insurance schemes and the income-related contribution under the Health Insurance Act; a pension scheme is only mandatory via an industry pension fund or collective agreement [2]. For 2026 the Tax Administration lists a low AWf (unemployment) premium of 2.74% and a high one of 7.74%. The low premium applies to a written contract for an indefinite period that isn't an on-call contract; a fixed-term contract therefore generally falls under the high premium. Exception: for an employee younger than 21 who is paid at most 48 hours per four weeks or 52 hours per month, the low premium always applies [3]. Which premium applies to you depends on the contract type and age; check with your payroll administration.

How does the risk differ between the two?

A fixed-term contract ends on the end date [4], whereas an employer can't simply terminate a permanent contract: for business-economic reasons or long-term incapacity for work, for example, UWV permission is needed [5]. In that respect a working-student contract is more flexible. But with a fixed-term contract too, a transition payment may be due on non-renewal: one third of the monthly wage per year of service [6], and the chain rule and notice obligation apply to fixed-term contracts [7][8]. The employer's notice period is, by law, one month for employment of less than five years [9].

When is a working student less cost-efficient than it looks?

If you underpay a working student relative to the level of the work, you save a few euros an hour on paper. Now work out what it costs when someone leaves: recruiting and onboarding all over again. Paying below the minimum wage isn't allowed anyway [1].

A simple calculation to run yourself

  • Work out the hourly rate separately from the number of hours: is it comparable to what a full-time junior colleague earns?
  • Count payroll taxes and contributions, including the difference between the low and high AWf premium for your contract type
  • Count recruiting and onboarding time, not just the salary
  • Include any transition payment on non-renewal
  • Look at the nature of the work: is it structural enough to justify a starter down the line, or does it fit better as fewer hours over a longer period?

Why this is more than a cost question

A working student isn't a price argument against a starter but a different way of organising the same kind of work, with a different risk profile. So don't choose on price, and be just as critical of underpayment as of the wrong contract type for the work at hand.
