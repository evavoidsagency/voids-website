SIDE JOB OR RELEVANT WORK EXPERIENCE: WHAT COUNTS MORE FOR YOUR CV?

Many students work alongside their studies to get by, not to gain experience. What that difference means for your start on the labour market, and how to close the gap.

This article is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Rules change and your situation may differ: check the current rules with an official source or a qualified advisor before relying on it.

Many students have a side job, often in retail or hospitality: working higher-education students in the third quarter of 2023 most often worked as sales assistants in retail (10%), in hospitality (8%) or as shelf stockers (5%) [1]. That work isn't lesser. It pays the bills and teaches you to take responsibility. But at your first application for a job in your field, it weighs differently from experience that connects to your studies.

Does a side job still count on your CV?

Yes, but differently from relevant experience. In a study by SEO Economic Research (2023), a side job during studies was associated with a shorter search for a first substantial job, and a side job in a relevant sector with an even shorter search than one in a non-relevant sector (in the study: hospitality, retail, events, postal and courier services, agriculture and temp work) [2]. A side job shows you can work and deal with customers or colleagues. Relevant experience also shows you know something about the field.

The problem: relevant experience isn't equally accessible

Relevant internships and working-student roles aren't always well paid. An internship allowance is not mandatory by law; in March 2024, 42% of secondary vocational students received one and an estimated 75% of students at universities of applied sciences, and in research universities 65% of students with a compulsory internship [3]. That makes unpaid or low-paid experience less accessible to students who also need to cover rent and groceries. Someone with a financial safety net can more easily afford it.

Paid and relevant don't have to be a trade-off

That you must choose between earning money and gaining experience isn't a law of nature. A working student has an employment contract and so a right to at least the minimum wage [4]. The cases VOIDS describes on its website concern paid working-student roles [5].

How do you spot a role that's both paid and relevant?

Watch for three things: a salary at or above the statutory minimum wage that isn't waved away with “experience is the reward”, tasks that clearly connect to your field of study or the work you're aiming for, and a contract with fixed hours rather than loose on-call shifts. If one is structurally missing, you're closer to a side job than a working-student role, whatever the vacancy calls it.

What you can do right now

  • Actively look for working-student roles instead of only side jobs, even while you're deep in your studies
  • Be critical about pay: relevant experience doesn't have to be unpaid
  • Need a side job right now to get by? Fine, but keep looking for something more relevant as soon as you can

Why this is the reason VOIDS exists

The gap between who can afford relevant experience and who can't is the core of why VOIDS exists. Access to work that matters shouldn't depend on who can afford to ask for little in return [6].
