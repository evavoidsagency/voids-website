GEN Z AND CHARACTER: WHAT DO YOUNG CANDIDATES LOOK AT?

A job title says little about daily reality. What CBS figures show about what young employees find important, and how VOIDS deals with it.

“Gen Z” is a label for a large and diverse group, so we'd rather look at numbers. What did young employees (aged 15 to 27) find important in a job in 2024? At the top: good managers, a good salary and job security; almost all young employees found those three (very) important [1]. Young people in education more often than others found it important to set their own working hours (84% versus 74%) and to work part-time (81% versus 68%). They less often found a permanent contract important (75% versus 91%) [1].

Why do job titles say so little?

“Marketing employee” can mean creating content at one company and mostly maintaining spreadsheets at another. Candidates mainly want to know how a team works, what the first month involves and who is responsible for what. Put that in your vacancy, next to the title.

What does a CV not tell you?

A CV shows what someone has done, but not how they work under pressure, with ambiguity or in a small team. That's why VOIDS weighs motivation and working style alongside experience and education [2].

This works both ways

Matching on character doesn't only mean companies look past a CV; it also means candidates look more critically at companies. Does the culture fit? Is there room to grow? Is the pay fair? Those are fair questions, and a good company has an answer.

Why leaning less on CV signals can be fairer

CV signals such as a well-known internship are associated with access to opportunities, and that access is not equally distributed. An internship allowance, for example, is not mandatory by law: in March 2024, 42% of secondary vocational students received one, an estimated 75% of students at universities of applied sciences and 65% of research-university students with a compulsory internship [3]. In a report on graduates, CBS refers to a 2021 SER advice in which students whose parents did not study, students with a secondary vocational background, with a non-Western migration background and with a functional impairment experience barriers in or on the way to higher education [4]. Leaning less on signals that presuppose access gives more candidates a chance.

Does this mean experience no longer counts?

No. Research by SEO Economic Research found that graduates with a side job in a relevant sector found a first substantial job faster on average [5]. Experience stays relevant. In a junior role, where a lot is new, we simply also look at how someone handles new situations.
